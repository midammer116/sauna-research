Are Saunas Bad for You

An evidence-based assessment of the potential risks and contraindications of sauna bathing for general and clinical populations.

Cardiovascular Safety Profile of Sauna Bathing

For the vast majority of healthy individuals, sauna bathing is remarkably safe. A comprehensive review of adverse events associated with sauna use published in the Mayo Clinic Proceedings found that serious complications are exceedingly rare. The cardiovascular demands of sauna bathing are comparable to moderate physical activity, and the transient increase in heart rate and decrease in blood pressure are well-tolerated by individuals without pre-existing unstable cardiovascular conditions.

Risks of Dehydration and Electrolyte Imbalance

The most common risk associated with sauna bathing is dehydration resulting from inadequate fluid intake before, during, or after a session. Sweat losses during a 15-minute sauna session can range from 0.5 to 1.0 liters. Failure to replace these fluids can lead to symptoms resembling mild heat exhaustion, including headache, dizziness, and fatigue. Electrolyte imbalances may also occur, particularly in individuals who are already sodium-depleted.

Thermal Stress and Heat-Related Complications

Although sauna temperatures typically range from 80 to 100 degrees Celsius, the dry heat is generally well-tolerated due to the body’s efficient thermoregulatory mechanisms. However, prolonged exposure beyond 20 minutes or sessions at excessively high temperatures can overwhelm these mechanisms, leading to heat exhaustion or heat stroke. Symptoms such as confusion, nausea, or loss of consciousness require immediate medical attention.

Respiratory Considerations and Contraindications

Sauna bathing is generally safe for individuals with well-controlled asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. However, individuals with acute respiratory infections or unstable asthma should avoid sauna use until symptoms have resolved. The dry air of a traditional sauna may occasionally trigger bronchospasm in sensitive individuals; those affected should consider lower temperatures or shorter sessions.

Pregnancy, Medications, and Special Populations

Pregnant women should exercise caution and consult their healthcare provider before using a sauna. While occasional short sessions at moderate temperatures appear safe, prolonged exposure to hyperthermia during the first trimester has been associated with an increased risk of neural tube defects in epidemiological studies. Individuals taking medications that affect thermoregulation, such as anticholinergics or beta-blockers, should also seek medical advice before regular sauna use.

Evidence-Based Safety Guidelines for Sauna Use

To minimize risk, the following evidence-based guidelines are recommended: limit individual sessions to 15 to 20 minutes at temperatures below 100 degrees Celsius. Hydrate adequately before and after each session. Avoid alcohol consumption before and during sauna use. Exit the sauna immediately if symptoms of dizziness, nausea, or chest discomfort develop. Individuals with known cardiovascular disease, unstable angina, or recent myocardial infarction should obtain medical clearance before initiating sauna bathing.
